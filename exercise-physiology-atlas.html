Core idea

Food does not directly contract muscle. Its chemical energy is used to rebuild ATP—the immediate currency every contraction spends.

01 / The energy problem

Every movement is an ATP turnover problem.

ATP stores are tiny, so the body must resynthesise ATP at almost exactly the rate you use it. “Aerobic” and “anaerobic” describe routes of resynthesis—not separate engines that switch on and off.1

⚡

ATP hydrolysis

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + H⁺ + energy

Myosin uses it for force; SERCA pumps use it to return Ca²⁺; Na⁺/K⁺ pumps restore electrical gradients.

↻

ATP resynthesis

ADP + Pᵢ + energy → ATP

Phosphocreatine, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation provide the energy. They overlap continuously.

≈

Power vs capacity

Fast pathways make ATP rapidly but run out quickly. Oxidative metabolism has lower maximum power, yet enormous capacity when fuel and oxygen delivery continue.

02 / The chemical routes

Three routes rebuild the same ATP.

Their inputs, speed, capacity and by-products differ. The cell blends them according to ATP demand, oxygen delivery, enzyme capacity, fibre recruitment and available fuel.

01 — fastest

Phosphagen

PCr + ADP + H⁺ ⇌ ATP + creatine

Creatine kinase transfers PCr’s phosphate directly to ADP. Very high power; very small reserve.

2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP

Adenylate kinase salvages another ATP; rising AMP also signals low cellular energy.

02 — fast

Glycolysis

Glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pᵢ + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 H₂O

Fast cytosolic ATP. Starting from muscle glycogen nets 3 ATP per glucose unit because the first phosphorylation cost was paid during storage.

03 — enduring

Oxidative phosphorylation

NADH/FADH₂ + O₂ + ADP + Pᵢ → NAD⁺/FAD + H₂O + ATP

Electrons power proton pumping across the inner mitochondrial membrane. ATP synthase lets protons flow back and couples that gradient to ATP formation.

Complete glucose oxidation

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ~30–32 ATP

The yield varies with NADH shuttles and coupling efficiency. “36–38 ATP” is an outdated oversimplification for human cells.

Palmitate oxidation

C₁₆H₃₂O₂ + 23 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 16 H₂O + ~106 ATP

Fat yields much more ATP per molecule but requires more oxygen per ATP and cannot match carbohydrate’s maximum ATP production rate.

Amino acids

Usually a small share of exercise energy. After nitrogen removal, carbon skeletons enter as pyruvate, acetyl-CoA or TCA intermediates.

Contribution rises with prolonged exercise, low glycogen, low energy availability or insufficient carbohydrate. Nitrogen ultimately leaves mainly as urea.

Lactate regenerates NAD⁺

Pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ ⇌ lactate + NAD⁺

This reaction keeps rapid glycolysis running. Lactate is exported through MCT transporters, oxidised by other fibres and the heart, or recycled into glucose by the liver.2

Lactate is not the acid villain

High glycolytic flux and ATP hydrolysis coincide with rising H⁺ and falling pH, but lactate formation itself consumes H⁺. Blood lactate rises when appearance exceeds clearance; it is a useful fuel and signal, not metabolic waste.2

And it does not cause next-day soreness—DOMS reflects exercise-induced tissue stress and inflammation.

03 / Whole-body transport

Oxygen travels. Carbon leaves. Heat must escape.

Endurance performance depends on a chain: ventilation, diffusion, blood oxygen carrying capacity, cardiac output, muscle blood flow, capillary exchange and mitochondrial use. A weak link limits the whole chain.

Training can raise delivery (especially stroke volume and plasma volume) and extraction (capillaries, mitochondria and oxidative enzymes).

A beautiful detail

The oxygen you breathe mostly becomes water—not carbon dioxide. CO₂ is released during pyruvate dehydrogenase and TCA-cycle decarboxylations. At the electron transport chain, O₂ accepts electrons and H⁺ to form H₂O.

NERVES

Immediate drive

Motor cortex recruits fibres; sympathetic activity raises heart rate, contractility and ventilation.

BLOOD FLOW

Local control

Metabolites and endothelial signals dilate vessels in active muscle, overriding sympathetic constriction locally.

FUELS

Mobilisation

Catecholamines support glycogenolysis and lipolysis; insulin usually falls while muscle contraction still increases glucose uptake.

COOLING

Heat loss

Skin blood flow and sweat rise. Evaporation cools; sweat that drips without evaporating removes little heat.

04 / Time and recruitment

Dominance changes by the second.

These are overlapping phases, not borders. Oxidative metabolism contributes from the first contraction but needs time to reach a high rate.

0–2 seconds

ATP already in the fibre buffers the instant demand.

Seconds

PCr supplies very high power; glycolysis accelerates immediately.

~10 s–2 min

Fast glycolysis can dominate hard efforts; oxidative share keeps climbing.

Minutes–hours

Oxidative ATP dominates sustained exercise; carb/fat mix shifts with intensity and stores.

Recovery

Oxygen supports PCr restoration, lactate oxidation, ion balance and later tissue remodelling.

Recruitment

The nervous system generally follows the size principle: low-threshold motor units first, then larger fast units as force demand or fatigue rises. A light load taken near failure can eventually recruit high-threshold units too.

05 / Adaptation

Training is a repeated molecular message.

A session disturbs energy, calcium, force and structure. Signalling changes gene expression and protein turnover; recovery builds a slightly better system. Consistency turns transient signals into anatomy.

Endurance signal

More delivery, extraction and durability

Ca²⁺+AMP/ATP ↑+ROS→AMPK / CaMK / p38→PGC-1α
  • Mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative enzymes
  • More capillaries and better endothelial function
  • Higher plasma volume and usually larger stroke volume
  • More glycogen storage and better fat oxidation at a given workload
  • Greater lactate transport, oxidation and threshold pace
  • Lower heart rate and fuel cost at the same submaximal pace

VO₂max can rise via central and peripheral changes; economy and sustainable fraction matter just as much in long events.3

Strength signal

More force, tissue and neural skill

Tension+strain→mechanosensors→mTORC1→MPS
  • Early neural gains: recruitment, rate coding and coordination
  • Myofibrillar protein accretion and fibre hypertrophy
  • Ribosome biogenesis supports higher protein-building capacity
  • Satellite cells can donate nuclei as fibres grow
  • Tendons and bone remodel more slowly than muscle
  • Higher strength reserve can improve endurance economy

Hypertrophy occurs when repeated increases in muscle protein synthesis accumulate beyond breakdown, with adequate training, energy and amino acids.

What changes quickly?

Plasma volume, enzymes, neural coordination, glycogen storage and signalling can change within days to weeks.

What changes slowly?

Meaningful hypertrophy, capillary growth, tendon stiffness, bone structure and major cardiac remodelling require repeated months and years.

Detraining

Plasma volume and some aerobic enzymes fade relatively fast; strength and muscle are better retained when load intensity remains, even if volume falls.

06 / The hybrid athlete

Cardio does not automatically kill gains.

Concurrent training can improve aerobic fitness and strength together. The practical “interference effect” is driven less by a single molecular switch and more by accumulated fatigue, high endurance volume, competing session quality and training the same muscle hard again before it recovers. Explosive strength appears more vulnerable than maximal strength or whole-muscle hypertrophy.4

KEEP

Two quality strength exposures

  • Squat or leg press; hinge; unilateral knee-dominant work
  • Calf/soleus; pull; push; trunk/bracing
  • Mostly 3–8 challenging reps for compounds; stop 1–3 reps before failure
  • Reduce sets before reducing load when race training peaks
PLACE

Protect key endurance sessions

  • Do not put exhaustive leg work immediately before key intervals or long runs
  • Pair strength with an easy endurance day when useful
  • If both sessions are hard, separate by several hours or different days
  • Running adds impact/eccentric stress—monitor it more carefully than easy cycling
KEEP

Lift while fresh

  • Do priority lifting before cardio in the same session
  • Use enough weekly sets to progress, then add endurance around them
  • Low-to-moderate cycling is easier to recover from than hard running
  • Do not turn every cardio session into a threshold test
PLACE

Build an aerobic base cheaply

  • Mostly conversational work; add a small dose of intervals
  • Separate hard lower-body endurance from hard leg lifting
  • Eat carbohydrate before sessions where power or volume matters
  • Track gym performance: persistent regression means recovery debt
Scheduling rule

Order the day around the adaptation you value most. Separation is a fatigue-management tool, not magic: more recovery between two hard same-muscle sessions usually preserves quality.

07 / Retaining muscle

Give muscle four reasons to stay.

The strongest defence is the combination of mechanical tension, enough amino acids, enough total energy and recovery. Endurance training raises—not removes—the need for protein.5

1

Keep loading

Lift at least twice weekly when possible. In high-volume phases, keep meaningful load and trim redundant sets.

2

Protein daily

A practical hybrid-athlete range is 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day, spread across 3–5 meals. Around 1.8 g/kg is a useful endurance anchor.56

3

Fuel quality

Carbohydrate protects training output and glycogen. Periodise it to workload; do not chronically underfuel hard training.

4

Recover

Sleep, easier days and sensible session spacing let the protein-building response exceed accumulated damage and breakdown.

Interpretation

Enough carbohydrate supports session quality; protein supplies amino acids for both contractile and mitochondrial remodelling.

Fuel by session demand

  • Short/easy: normal meals may be enough
  • 1–2.5 h: often 30–60 g carbohydrate/hour
  • >2.5 h: often 60–90 g/hour using multiple transportable carbohydrates, after gut training
  • Rapid turnaround: carbohydrate immediately matters more when another glycogen-demanding session follows soon

Ranges come from sports-nutrition position guidance; individual tolerance and race intensity matter.7

Low energy availability warning

Chronic underfueling can impair reproductive hormones, bone, immunity, iron status, protein synthesis, mood and performance—collectively described within RED-S.8

  • Persistent fatigue, irritability or declining output
  • Frequent illness or slow healing
  • Menstrual disruption or low libido
  • Bone stress injury or recurrent pain
  • Feeling cold, poor sleep, obsessional food/training behaviour

These signs deserve professional assessment; there is no single universal calorie threshold that diagnoses RED-S.

Hydration

Replace enough fluid and sodium to preserve performance, but do not force fluid beyond losses. Body-mass gain during a long event is a warning for overdrinking and exercise-associated hyponatraemia.

08 / Clean mental models

Five myths to delete.

×

“Anaerobic means zero oxygen.”

It means ATP demand is being met substantially by non-oxygen-dependent routes. Oxygen use continues and often rises.

×

“Lactate causes next-day soreness.”

Lactate clears quickly and is useful fuel. DOMS develops later from unfamiliar loading and tissue/inflammatory responses.

×

“Fat-burning zone means most fat loss.”

Lower intensities use a higher fraction of fat, but long-term fat loss depends mainly on total energy balance and adherence.

×

“Sweating means burning fat.”

Sweat loss is water and electrolytes. Scale weight returns with rehydration.

×

“Cardio destroys muscle.”

Underrecovery and underfueling do. Sensibly programmed endurance and resistance work are compatible.

×

“Breathing O₂ creates the CO₂.”

O₂ becomes water at the electron transport chain. CO₂ comes mainly from carbon-fuel decarboxylation upstream.

10 / Fast glossary

Terms worth owning.

ATP, ADP and Pᵢ

Adenosine triphosphate, adenosine diphosphate and inorganic phosphate. ATP hydrolysis releases usable free energy; metabolism reattaches phosphate to ADP.

Oxidation and reduction

Oxidation loses electrons; reduction gains them. Fuels are oxidised, NAD⁺/FAD are reduced, and oxygen is finally reduced to water.

Glycolysis and glycogenolysis

Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate. Glycogenolysis releases glucose units from stored glycogen so they can enter glycolysis.

β-oxidation

The mitochondrial spiral that cuts fatty acids into two-carbon acetyl-CoA units while generating NADH and FADH₂.

Lactate threshold

An intensity region where blood lactate rises more rapidly because appearance increasingly exceeds clearance. It is not one universal switch and depends on the testing method.

VO₂max and economy

VO₂max is the maximum rate of oxygen use. Economy is the oxygen/energy cost at a given speed or power. Long performance also depends on the fraction of VO₂max you can sustain.

MPS and MPB

Muscle protein synthesis and breakdown. Net balance is MPS minus MPB; repeated positive remodelling plus training can accumulate muscle.

AMPK, PGC-1α and mTORC1

AMPK senses cellular energy stress; PGC-1α coordinates many mitochondrial adaptations; mTORC1 regulates growth-related protein synthesis. Real concurrent adaptation is a network, not a simple one-switch battle.
